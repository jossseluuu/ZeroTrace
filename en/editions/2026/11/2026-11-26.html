The tablet the whole family uses shouldn't be signed in to your account

Why a shared iPad or Android tablet signed in to your personal account shows your messages and verification codes to whoever picks it up, how to separate it in ten minutes, and what you don't need to do.

In a nutshell

It happens all the time: one parent set up the family tablet with their own Apple or Google account, and now the kids, the grandparents, or visiting friends use it. It feels convenient, but to Apple and Google that tablet isn't "the family one": it's just another one of your devices.

That means it can show whatever you sync to your account. On an iPad, Apple treats any device where you've signed in with as a : your account's verification codes show up there. And if you turned on text message forwarding from your iPhone, your texts arrive too, including codes from your bank.

The fix isn't to stop sharing the tablet. It's to separate: give the tablet its own account (a child account if a kid uses it), use separate Android users when the model supports them, and require approval for purchases. It takes about ten minutes, once.

The essentials

  • To Apple and Google, a tablet signed in to your account is your device: it shows your messages and verification codes.
  • If a kid uses it, create a child account with Family Sharing (Apple) or Family Link (Google) instead of lending yours.
  • On Android, add a user or a guest in Settings > System > Multiple users: they can't see your messages or photos.
  • Turn on purchase approval: Ask to Buy on Apple and Purchase verification: Always on Google Play.

Real-world risk

Likelihood: medium

No attacker required: someone else just has to use the tablet and see what comes in. Someone with bad intentions taking advantage of it is less common, but the people who have it handy are usually people you trust.

Impact: medium

Private messages, photos, and verification codes in plain view, plus purchases nobody approved. If someone also has your password, the tablet gives them the second factor.

How it works

What makes a tablet a "trusted device"

Apple defines a trusted device as an iPhone, iPad, Apple Watch, Apple Vision Pro, or Mac where you've already signed in to your Apple Account using two-factor authentication. The six-digit code automatically appears on those devices when someone tries to sign in to your account somewhere else.

Apple adds that trusted devices can also approve important account changes, such as a password change. In other words, the living-room iPad signed in to your account doesn't just "see" things: it carries weight in your account's security.

Your texts, on the tablet

Your iPhone can forward SMS, MMS, and RCS messages to other devices signed in to the same Apple Account from Settings > Apps > Messages > Text Message Forwarding. If you turned the iPad on in that list, the codes your bank texts you show up there too.

It isn't a bug: it's a feature meant for people who use several devices of their own. The problem starts when that device stops being only yours.

Android has user profiles; use them

Android supports multiple users on one device (Settings > System > Multiple users). According to Google, each user has their own files and messages (texts, email, photos, music) and can't see data from apps another user installed. Some settings, like Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, are shared.

There's also a guest profile, meant for someone using the device for a short time, and a restricted profile that Google says is available on tablets only. Samsung also supports multiple users on Galaxy tablets, though menus vary by model.

Purchases: the old problem

In 2014, the FTC required Apple to refund at least $32.5 million for in-app purchases kids made without permission, after the company received tens of thousands of complaints. Today both Apple and Google have dedicated controls for this: Ask to Buy for Apple child accounts and Google Play's purchase verification.

What an attack looks like

  1. The starting point

    The family tablet is signed in to your personal account and, on an iPad, text message forwarding from your iPhone is on. Half the household knows the passcode.

  2. Someone picks it up

    A relative, an ex, a guest, or whoever finds it if the passcode is easy. No special software needed: opening Messages or glancing at notifications is enough.

  3. They see what comes in

    Private messages, synced photos and, above all, codes: your Apple Account verification codes and texts from your bank or other services.

  4. The worst case

    If that person already has your password (through a scam or because they saw it), the tablet gives them the second step they were missing to get into your account or confirm a transaction.

  5. The defense

    Take your account off the tablet. Apple confirms that a device removed from your account no longer displays verification codes.

What an attacker can and can't do

Can

  • Read the messages and see the photos you sync to your account on that tablet.
  • See verification codes that reach the tablet as a trusted device or through forwarded texts.
  • Make app store purchases with your payment method if there's no approval or verification.
  • Use the tablet as a second factor if they already know your password.

Can't

  • See any of this if the tablet uses a different account or, on Android, a different user: according to Google, users don't share files or messages.
  • Receive codes on a tablet you've removed from your account.
  • Get around Ask to Buy: the purchase stays pending until the adult approves it from their own device.
  • Leave a locked app (Guided Access or App pinning) without the passcode or PIN.

What to do

If a kid uses it, give them their own child account

  • Impact: high
  • Effort: medium
  • ⏱ 15 min
  • For: everyone

This is real separation. Apple requires a parent or guardian to create accounts for children under 13 (age varies by country), and Google lets you create and supervise a child's account with Family Link.

iPad

  1. On your iPhone or iPad, go to Settings > Family > Add Member > Create Child Account > Continue and follow the steps.
  2. Sign in to the tablet with that child account instead of yours.

Android

  1. Install Family Link on your phone and create or add your child's Google Account.
  2. Set up the tablet with that account.

Take your account off the shared tablet

  • Impact: high
  • Effort: low
  • ⏱ 5 min
  • For: everyone

That way it stops being a trusted device and stops receiving your codes. Apple recommends signing out on the device itself so it doesn't reappear in your list.

iPad

  1. On the tablet itself, open Settings and sign out of your Apple Account.
  2. If it isn't handy: on your iPhone, go to Settings > [your name], scroll down to the device list, tap the tablet, and tap Remove from Account.

On Android, one user per person (or a guest)

  • Impact: high
  • Effort: low
  • ⏱ 10 min
  • For: everyone

If the tablet is yours but others use it often, create a separate user. For occasional use, a guest is easy to delete afterward. Only the device owner can add users.

Android

  1. Go to Settings > System > Multiple users > Add user > OK and enter a name (if you don't see it: Add user or profile > User).
  2. To switch users, swipe down from the top and tap the user icon. On Samsung tablets, the menu is under Settings > Accounts and backup > Users.

Check text message forwarding on your iPhone

  • Impact: medium
  • Effort: low
  • ⏱ 1 min
  • For: everyone

Leave on only the devices you use yourself.

iPhone

  1. Go to Settings > Apps > Messages > Text Message Forwarding.
  2. Turn off the shared tablet.

Put purchases behind approval

  • Impact: medium
  • Effort: low
  • ⏱ 3 min
  • For: everyone

That way no purchase goes through without an adult approving it.

iPad

  1. With a child account: Settings > Family > [name] > Apps & Websites > Restrictions > Ask to Buy and turn on Require Purchase Approval.

Android

  1. In Google Play, tap your profile picture > Payments & subscriptions > Purchase verification and choose Always, not Every 30 minutes or Never.

Lock the app before handing it overDeep-dive level

  • Impact: low
  • Effort: low
  • ⏱ 2 min
  • For: advanced

For lending the tablet for a while (a video, a game) without anyone leaving that app.

iPad

  1. Turn on Settings > Accessibility > Guided Access and set a passcode.
  2. Open the app and triple-click the top button (or the Home button on older models). To end, triple-click, enter the passcode, and tap End.

Android

  1. Turn on Settings > Security > Advanced > App pinning (it may be under Security & location).
  2. Unpinning will require your PIN, pattern, or password.

Check it yourself

Which account is the tablet using?

Open Settings on the tablet and check the name at the top. If it's yours and you're not the only one using it, follow the steps above.

Is it in your device list?

On your iPhone, Settings > [your name]: scroll down to the list and make sure only your own devices are there.

Does it get your texts?

In Text Message Forwarding, the shared tablet shouldn't be turned on.

Does it ask before buying?

Try downloading a paid app on the tablet: it should ask for approval or your verification.

What you don't need to do

Buying a tablet for every family member

With its own account, or separate users on Android, sharing a tablet is perfectly reasonable.

Third-party parental control apps "just in case"

Separating accounts and approving purchases only takes the built-in tools: Family Sharing and Screen Time on Apple, Family Link on Google.

Factory resetting the tablet

To take your account off, signing out or removing it from your account is enough. A reset is only needed if you're selling or giving it away.

Turning off two-factor authentication to stop codes on the tablet

That would weaken your security to fix a different problem. The right move is for the tablet to stop being a trusted device.

Myth and reality

What people think

"If the store asks for my password, my kid can't buy anything without me."

What actually happens

It depends on your settings. In 2014 the FTC found that entering the password once opened a 15-minute window of purchases without asking again. Today Google Play still offers verification every 30 minutes: if you type it in front of your kid, they can keep buying for that long. Choose Always or use a child account with approval.

FTC: Apple will provide full consumer refunds of at least $32.5 million (2014)

For technical readers

User isolation on Android

Each secondary Android user gets its own storage and its own app instances; what's shared are updates to apps installed by more than one user and some system settings, like Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. The restricted profile (tablets only) derives from the primary user and limits which apps and content it sees. Only the device owner can create or delete users, and on Samsung the admin account needs a screen lock before creating a restricted profile.

Trusted devices and their reach

In Apple's model, the second factor is "something you have": the devices where you've already authenticated with two-factor authentication. Every device you add widens the surface: anyone with the passcode to any of them can see a verification code or approve changes. Reviewing the device list regularly (on your iPhone or at account.apple.com > Devices) keeps it to a minimum. If you manage several tablets at a school or business, the right tool is mobile device management (MDM), not personal accounts.

Guided Access and App pinning

Both features lock the device to a single app. On iPad, Guided Access can also disable buttons, motion, the keyboard, or areas of the screen and set a time limit; Apple notes that Crash Detection and emergency calls aren't available during a session. On Android, when the feature is on, leaving a pinned app requires the screen lock. Neither replaces a separate account: they only limit one session.

Glossary

Trusted device
A device where you've already signed in to your account using two-factor authentication. It displays verification codes and can approve account changes.
Two-factor authentication
On top of your password, a second piece of information is required, usually a code that appears on one of your devices or arrives by text.
