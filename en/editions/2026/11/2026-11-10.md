# That bargain Android tablet may come with a stowaway installed at the factory

> What an "uncertified" tablet is, why some arrive with a backdoor already on board, and the one-minute check to do before and after you buy.

Tuesday, November 10, 2026 · 📲 Tablets / iPad · Reading time: 3 min (Deep dive: 9 min)

## Today's status: Quiet day

**No new alerts today.** Nothing published in the last few hours meets our bar: real-world exploitation or broad impact on everyday devices. Earlier alerts are still in the log.

## In a nutshell

An off-brand Android tablet for far less than usual looks like the perfect gift for the kids or for streaming shows in the kitchen. Cheap isn't the problem. The problem is that some of these tablets **leave the factory with malware already installed**, before anyone ever turns them on.

The best-known family is called BADBOX 2.0. The FBI says it involves **millions of infected devices** (TV streaming boxes, projectors, picture frames and, researchers say, tablets too); Google puts the figure at more than 10 million and took the operators to court in July 2025. The device works normally, but behind the scenes it uses your connection for ad fraud and rents out your internet address to strangers.

Almost all of them share one trait: **they aren't Google Play Protect certified**. That certification means the model passed Google's security and compatibility testing, and you can check it in about a minute from the Play Store. With Black Friday and holiday shopping just around the corner, now's the time to look.

**The essentials**

- Before buying an Android tablet, check that the brand is on Google's official list of certified partners.
- Be wary of tablets advertised as "unlocked," with "free content," or from brands you don't recognize.
- On the tablet you already own: Play Store > profile icon > Settings > About, under Play Protect certification.
- If it isn't certified, return it if you can; a factory reset won't clean it.

**Real-world risk**

- **Likelihood: low.** Tablets from well-known brands bought at regular stores are certified. The risk is concentrated in very cheap models from unknown brands, which are also sold on major online marketplaces.
- **Impact: medium.** Your connection can end up carrying fraud and other people's traffic under your IP address, and everything you do on that tablet (accounts, passwords) sits on a device someone else controls.

## How it works

### There's plenty of Android, but not all of it is certified

Android is open source: anyone can take the Android Open Source Project (AOSP) code and build their own tablet. To ship the Play Store and Google apps, the manufacturer has to pass Google's compatibility testing and earn **Play Protect certification**.

Google's help page is blunt: if a device isn't certified, Google **has no record of its compatibility test results**, it may not be secure, and it may not get updates. And if it comes with Google apps, those apps aren't licensed: **they aren't real Google apps**.

### How the stowaway gets in

HUMAN Security, the firm that exposed BADBOX 2.0 in March 2025, describes three routes: the backdoor comes **preinstalled** on the system, it's **downloaded on first boot** from a criminal server, or it arrives with **apps from third-party stores** that the user installs.

HUMAN found it on more than a million devices across **222 countries and territories**, all running uncertified open-source Android and manufactured in mainland China. The FBI issued a public service announcement on June 5, 2025, and EFF notes affected devices have been sold out of the box through major online retailers.

### What they want it for

The criminals care less about your tablet than about **your connection**. According to HUMAN, they sell access to your IP address as a residential proxy, load hidden ads no one sees, and fake clicks to collect fraudulent ad revenue. It all happens in the background while your kid watches cartoons.

The risk to you is twofold: your internet address shows up in third-party activity you don't control, and because the device is part of a botnet, it can receive new commands or new apps whenever its operators want.

### Why a factory reset doesn't help

When the malicious code ships as part of the system, **a factory reset leaves it right where it was**: "factory" already includes the stowaway. That's also why antivirus isn't the answer. The FBI advises assessing every connected device and keeping firmware up to date, so it's worth asking the seller or manufacturer about an update, but don't count on one.

The realistic way out is usually a return. Check the retailer's return policy and act before the window closes.

## What an attack looks like

1. **At the factory.** A manufacturer or a supplier in its chain plants the backdoor in the system image of a cheap model built on open-source Android, without Google certification.
2. **On the virtual shelf.** The tablet sells online at an irresistible price, sometimes with pitches like "unlocked" or "free content." You don't recognize the brand, but the reviews look good.
3. **First boot.** Once it joins your Wi-Fi, it contacts the criminals' server and downloads the rest of its modules. All you see is a tablet that works.
4. **Quiet use.** While you use it, it resells your connection as a proxy, loads invisible ads, and generates fake clicks. It may run a little slower or warmer, nothing more.
5. **The fallout.** Your IP shows up in other people's fraud, and the device keeps waiting for orders. Any accounts you signed in to on it now live on a system you don't control.

## What an attacker can and can't do

**Can**

- Use your internet connection as an exit point for other people's traffic.
- Load hidden ads and fake clicks in the background.
- Install new apps or modules without asking you.
- See what you do on that tablet, because it's part of the system.

**Can't**

- Jump through the air to your phone or laptop: it arrives with the device or with apps from shady stores.
- Do anything offline: no internet, no fraud and no remote control.
- Touch accounts you've never signed in to on that tablet.
- Simply install its apps on a certified Android device: Google updated Play Protect to block BADBOX-associated apps.

## What to do

### Check the brand before you buy

`Impact: high` `Effort: low` `⏱ 3 min` `For: everyone`

Google publishes the list of **partners that ship certified phones and tablets**. It lists brands, not models, but if the brand isn't there, don't buy it. The FBI adds red flags: unrecognizable brands, devices advertised as "unlocked" or offering free content, and being told to turn off Play Protect.

**Before you pay**

1. Look up the brand at android.com/certified/partners.
2. Make sure the listing mentions Google Play, not a proprietary app store.
3. If the price is far below well-known brands with similar specs, be suspicious.

### Check certification on the one you own

`Impact: high` `Effort: low` `⏱ 1 min` `For: everyone`

This is the check Google and the FBI both recommend. Do it on Android TV boxes and Android-based projectors too.

**Android**

1. Open **Google Play Store** and tap your profile icon at the top right.
2. Go to **Settings** > **About**.
3. Find the **Play Protect certification** entry: if it says "Device is not certified," it isn't.

### Check the version and patch level

`Impact: medium` `Effort: low` `⏱ 1 min` `For: everyone`

A new tablet with a very old Android version or a years-old security patch is a bad sign. EFF observed that Android 9 through 12 were the most common versions among affected devices.

**Android**

1. Open **Settings** > **About tablet** > **Android version**.
2. Check the **Android version** and the **Android security update** date.
3. Look for updates in **Settings** > **System** > **Software updates**.

### If it isn't certified

`Impact: high` `Effort: medium` `⏱ 15 min` `For: everyone`

Turning Play Protect off or on won't fix an uncertified device, and a reset won't clean it. The sensible move is to get it off your network.

**Now**

1. If you're within the return window, send it back.
2. If not, contact the seller or manufacturer and ask whether a fixed update exists.
3. In the meantime, disconnect it from Wi-Fi and don't sign in to your email, bank, or social accounts on it.
4. If you already used important accounts on it, change those passwords from another device.
5. You can report suspected compromise to the FBI at ic3.gov.

### Park questionable devices on the guest network

`Impact: medium` `Effort: medium` `⏱ 10 min` `For: advanced`

If you decide to keep using an uncertified device (a projector, a TV box), connect it to your router's **guest network** so it can't see your other devices. The FBI also recommends monitoring your home network traffic.

**Router**

1. Turn on the guest network in your router's admin page.
2. Connect only the devices you don't trust to it.
3. Check in the router which devices use more data than usual when nobody's using them.

## Check it yourself

- **Is my tablet certified?.** Play Store > profile icon > **Settings** > **About**. If you see "Device is not certified," it isn't.
- **Is this brand trustworthy?.** Look it up on Android's official list of certified partners. If it's not there, whatever app store it ships with isn't Google's, even if it looks like it.
- **Is it acting strange?.** It runs warm or uses data while idle, apps you didn't install show up, or it asks you to turn off Play Protect. No single sign proves it, but together they're reason to unplug it.

## What you don't need to do

- **Distrusting every Android tablet.** Certified brands get Play Protect and updates. The problem is uncertified models, not Android.
- **Installing antivirus to "clean" it.** If the stowaway lives in the system, antivirus can't remove it. Spend that time on the return instead.
- **Checking certification every week.** Certification belongs to the model and doesn't change on its own. Checking once when you set up the tablet is enough.
- **Swearing off big online retailers.** The risk is in the product, not the store. Look at the brand and the certification, wherever you shop.

## Myth and reality

- **What people think:** "If it has the Play Store, Google must have approved it."
- **What actually happens:** Google warns that on uncertified devices, Google apps **aren't licensed and aren't real Google apps**, and EFF points out that preloaded app stores can mimic legitimate ones. What counts is the certification status under **Settings** > **About** in the Play Store. ([Google Play Help: Check & fix Play Protect certification status](https://support.google.com/googleplay/answer/7165974?hl=en))

## For technical readers

### AOSP vs. certified Android

Play Protect certification builds on Android compatibility testing and is the prerequisite for distributing Google's services. An uncertified AOSP device isn't necessarily malicious, but **no one has vetted its system image** and there's no guarantee of patches.

Google also notes that a device with an unlocked bootloader or root access fails certification checks. It's an integrity signal, not a quality seal for the manufacturer.

### A supply chain attack

BADBOX is a textbook supply chain attack: the malicious code gets in before the user receives the device, in a system partition the user can't modify. According to HUMAN, version 2.0 combines the preinstalled backdoor with modules pulled from a command-and-control server on first boot.

For defenders, the practical upshot is that scanning installed apps isn't enough: you have to trust the system image. That trust comes from certification, not from antivirus installed afterward.

### If you manage networks or a fleet of tablets

Require certified devices in procurement and check certification at enrollment. Put unmanaged consumer devices on an isolated VLAN or SSID and watch for unusual outbound connections, especially steady proxy traffic from devices that should be idle.

## Glossary

- **BADBOX 2.0:** Backdoor malware that arrives preinstalled, or on first boot, on cheap uncertified Android devices and enlists them in a criminal network.
- **AOSP:** Android Open Source Project: the free version of Android any manufacturer can use. Without certification, it doesn't include Google's apps or protections.
- **Backdoor:** Hidden code that lets someone control a device remotely without its owner knowing.
- **Residential proxy:** A service that routes other people's traffic through a home internet connection so it looks like an ordinary user's.
- **Botnet:** A network of infected devices that a criminal controls all at once to commit fraud or attacks.
- **Supply chain attack:** An attack that compromises a product during manufacturing or distribution, before it reaches the user.
