Who can turn on your laptop's camera and microphone

A sticker over the camera feels reassuring, but permissions are what actually decide who can see and hear you. How to review them in five minutes, and what to do if you get the "I recorded you" email.

In a nutshell

Your laptop doesn't let just any program use the camera or microphone: a list of permissions decides, built up every time you (or whoever installed the apps) clicked "Allow" without much thought.

That list is what you need to review. A video-calling app you no longer use, a website you allowed once, or a program you don't remember installing may still have access. A camera cover doesn't cover the microphone, and the microphone is exactly what people forget.

And if you get an email saying someone recorded you through your webcam and you have to pay in Bitcoin, take a breath: the FTC has been warning for years that it's a mass scam. Even if it includes one of your passwords, that password came from an old data breach, not from your camera.

The essentials

  • Check which apps have camera and microphone access, and remove the ones you don't use.
  • Do the same for the websites you've allowed in your browser.
  • Watch the camera light and the microphone icon: they tell you when they're in use.
  • If you get an "I recorded you" email, don't pay or reply; if it shows one of your passwords, change it.

Real-world risk

Likelihood: low

Secretly turning on your camera takes malware installed on the computer, or an app with permission that abuses it. That's uncommon on an up-to-date machine; the fake blackmail email, on the other hand, reaches huge numbers of people.

Impact: high

Private images or conversations in someone else's hands are hard to take back and can be used for blackmail. That's why a five-minute review is worth it.

How it works

Who decides whether an app can use the camera

Windows and macOS each have a section for the camera and another for the microphone. The first time an app asks, the system prompts you, and your answer is saved until you change it.

On macOS, every app appears in the list and you turn it on or off individually, for both camera and microphone.

On Windows there are two kinds of programs. Microsoft Store apps show up in a list and are controlled one by one. Desktop apps (your browser, Teams, Zoom, and most programs you install from a website) can't be controlled individually: a single switch allows or blocks all of them, and Windows lists below it the ones it has detected using the camera or microphone. Microsoft notes that some may not appear in the list.

The lights and icons that warn you

On a Mac, a green light next to the camera glows when it's on. Apple says the camera is engineered so that it can't activate without that light also turning on.

For the microphone, macOS shows a in Control Center when the mic is in use or has been used recently.

On Windows, if your device has a camera light, it turns on when the camera is active; if not, Windows shows a notification when the camera turns on or off. When an app is using the microphone, a microphone icon appears in the notification area of the taskbar.

Your browser has its own permissions

A website can never use your camera without asking the browser for permission. But if you once said yes for a video call, that permission may still be saved for that site.

All major browsers show an icon in the tab or address bar while a site is using the camera or microphone, and let you see and remove each site's permissions from the icon to the left of the address.

The "I recorded you" email, and why it's a lie

It's one of the most common scams: an email claims your computer was hacked, that you were recorded through the camera, and that the video goes to your friends and family within hours unless you pay in Bitcoin. The FTC's verdict on those claims of computer or webcam access: it's all talk.

To seem credible, the email sometimes includes a password you actually used. The FTC explains it usually comes from a at some service: it doesn't prove anyone got into your computer, but it does mean that password is exposed and needs changing.

What an attack looks like

  1. Getting in

    The attacker needs to run something on your computer: a pirated program with a surprise inside, an attachment you open, or a "player" a website asks you to install. That's how they plant a .

  2. The permission

    On macOS, that program would have to get past the permission prompt or abuse an app that already has access. On Windows, if it's a desktop program and desktop apps are allowed, no new prompt appears.

  3. The recording

    It turns on the camera or microphone when it thinks you're not looking. On a Mac, the green light will come on; on Windows, the light will if the device has one, and the microphone icon will show up in the taskbar.

  4. The blackmail

    With that material, the attacker can attempt real . That's far rarer than the fake email version, which needs no access to your computer at all.

What an attacker can and can't do

Can

  • Use the camera and microphone if they manage to install malware and it gets permission.
  • Take advantage of permission an app or website you once approved already has.
  • Listen through the microphone even if the camera is covered.
  • Scare you with a fake email that includes one of your passwords taken from a breach.

Can't

  • Turn on the camera of an up-to-date computer over the internet without first getting something to run on it.
  • Turn on a Mac's camera without the green light coming on, according to the design Apple describes.
  • Make a website use your camera without the browser asking you for permission.
  • Prove they recorded you just by showing you an old password.

What to do

Check which apps can use the camera and microphone

  • Impact: high
  • Effort: low
  • ⏱ 3 min
  • For: everyone

This is the step that actually closes the door. Remove access from anything you don't use for calls or recordings; if you ever need it, the app will ask again.

Windows 11

  1. Start > Settings > Privacy & security > Camera.
  2. In the app list, turn off the ones that don't need the camera.
  3. Below, under Let desktop apps access your camera, see which programs Windows has detected. If one doesn't ring a bell, look into it or uninstall it.
  4. Repeat under Privacy & security > Microphone.

Mac

  1. Apple menu > System Settings > Privacy & Security > Camera.
  2. Turn off the apps that don't need it.
  3. Repeat under Privacy & Security > Microphone.

Clean up website permissions

  • Impact: medium
  • Effort: low
  • ⏱ 2 min
  • For: everyone

A video-call site you used once doesn't need to keep access. Removing it breaks nothing: it'll just ask again next time.

Any browser

  1. Open the site and click the icon to the left of the address to see its permissions.
  2. In your browser settings, find the site permissions for Camera and Microphone and remove any you don't recognize.

Safari (Mac)

  1. Safari > Settings > Websites > Camera (and Microphone).

Learn to read the signals

  • Impact: medium
  • Effort: low
  • ⏱ habit
  • For: everyone

The light and the icon are your alarm. If they come on when you're not on a call, find out which app it is and revoke its access.

Windows

  1. Camera light (if your device has one) or a notification when it turns on.
  2. Microphone icon in the notification area of the taskbar.

Mac

  1. Green light next to the camera.
  2. Recording Indicator in Control Center for the microphone.

If you cover the camera, keep the cover thin

  • Impact: low
  • Effort: low
  • ⏱ 1 min
  • For: everyone

A cover is fine as an extra, but Apple warns that closing a MacBook with a cover thicker than a sheet of printer paper (0.1 mm) can damage the display, and that it may block the ambient light sensor.

Always

  1. Use a cover no thicker than 0.1 mm, or remove it before closing the lid.
  2. Avoid adhesives that leave residue on the camera.

If you get the "I recorded you" email

  • Impact: high
  • Effort: low
  • ⏱ 5 min
  • For: everyone

The right response is the opposite of what the email wants: no rushing and no paying.

Always

  1. Don't pay and don't reply. Delete the message.
  2. If it includes one of your passwords, change it everywhere you use it and turn on two-step verification.
  3. Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

Turn off the camera system-wideDeep-dive level

  • Impact: medium
  • Effort: medium
  • ⏱ 1 min
  • For: advanced

If you rarely make video calls, you can cut off every app and turn access back on only when you need it.

Windows 11

  1. Privacy & security > Camera > turn off Camera access.
  2. Keep in mind Windows Hello can still use the camera to sign you in.

Mac

  1. Under Privacy & Security > Camera, leave every app turned off and switch one on only while you use it.

Check it yourself

The Windows desktop apps list

Under Privacy & security > Camera (and > Microphone), the desktop apps section shows the programs Windows has detected using them. A name you don't recognize is your best clue that something doesn't belong.

Your Mac's permission lists

In System Settings > Privacy & Security, review Camera and Microphone. Every app listed there has asked for access at some point: compare the list with what you actually use.

A quick test

Open your system's camera app and check that the light comes on or the notification appears. That way you'll know what the signal looks like on your computer and recognize it when it shows up unexpectedly.

What you don't need to do

Buying "webcam protection" software

Your system's permissions and the light and icon signals already do that job. What matters is keeping your system updated and not installing programs from sketchy sources.

Unplugging or uninstalling the camera

No need to break anything. If you don't use it, removing permissions or turning off camera access system-wide is enough.

Reviewing permissions every week

Doing it today and again whenever you install a video-calling app or a new program is plenty.

Paying the blackmailer "just in case"

Paying deletes nothing, because there's nothing to delete, and it marks you as someone who pays. The useful step is changing any password the email shows.

Myth and reality

What people think

"If the blackmail email shows my password, they must have hacked my camera."

What actually happens

That password usually comes from an old data breach at some service. It proves your password is exposed, not that anyone got into your computer or recorded you. The FTC is blunt: don't pay anything, delete the message, it's a scam. Then change that password.

For technical readers

Packaged apps vs. desktop apps on Windows

Microsoft Store apps run with a package identity and declare the capabilities they need, such as camera or microphone. That's why Windows can list and control them one by one.

Traditional desktop apps don't have that identity, so Windows only offers one shared switch for all of them plus a list of the ones it has detected. Microsoft notes that desktop apps won't appear in the lists of apps you can turn on and off, and that to allow or block them you use each application's own settings. In practice, your best defense on Windows is not installing desktop programs you don't need.

Why the Mac light is a design guarantee

Apple says the camera in its laptops is engineered so it can't activate without the indicator light also turning on. That makes the light a reliable signal rather than just a software notice.

On Windows, the light depends on how each manufacturer implemented it. So on those machines, also rely on the notification and on the list of apps Windows has detected.

What changes if the attacker is already an administrator

Privacy permissions protect against ordinary apps. Malware that has already gained administrator privileges has many more ways to get around system controls. That's why the basics still apply: an updated system, a standard account for everyday use, and no programs from sketchy sources.

The camera in the browser

Websites reach the camera and microphone through the getUserMedia API, which browsers only expose in secure contexts (HTTPS) and always after explicit permission, stored per origin. If you build websites, request permission only when the user clicks the call button, not on page load: it's more respectful and makes it more likely they'll say yes.

Glossary

Privacy permission
An authorization the operating system stores for each app that decides whether it can use the camera, microphone, or other sensitive resources.
Recording Indicator
A signal in the Mac's Control Center showing that the microphone is in use or has been used recently.
Data breach
Theft or exposure of a service's database, including users' emails and passwords, which then circulate among criminals.
Remote access trojan (RAT)
Malware that lets an attacker control a computer remotely: view the screen, steal files, or turn on the camera and microphone.
Sextortion
Blackmail that threatens to share intimate images, real or made up, unless the victim pays.
